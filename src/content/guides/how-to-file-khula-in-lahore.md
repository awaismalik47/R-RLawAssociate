Khula is the right of a Muslim wife in Pakistan to ask the court to dissolve her marriage. This guide explains **how to file khula in Lahore**, step by step: who can file, which court to go to, what documents you need, what happens at each hearing and what you must do after the decree. It is written for general information; every case has its own facts, and you should speak to an advocate before acting.

## What khula means

In Islamic law, a marriage can be ended in several ways. **Talaq** is divorce pronounced by the husband. **Khula** is dissolution sought by the wife, usually in return for giving up some or all of the dower (**haq mehr**) she received. If the husband does not agree to end the marriage, the wife can ask the Family Court to grant khula.

Pakistani courts have long recognised that a wife does not need to prove fault to obtain khula. If she satisfies the court that she cannot live with her husband "within the limits prescribed by Allah", meaning that the marriage has broken down to the point where she cannot continue it, the court can dissolve the marriage. This is different from a suit for dissolution under the **Dissolution of Muslim Marriages Act 1939**, where the wife relies on specific grounds such as cruelty, failure to maintain her for two years, or desertion. Both claims are often made in the same suit.

## Who can file and where

Any married Muslim woman can file a suit for khula. You do not need your husband's consent and you do not need to wait for any particular period after the marriage.

In Lahore, the suit is filed in the **Family Court**. Family Courts sit at the Lahore district courts and in other court complexes in the city. Under the Family Courts Rules, a wife can file in the court within whose area she **ordinarily resides**, even if the marriage took place elsewhere or the husband lives in another city. This is a significant practical benefit: a woman living with her parents in Lahore can usually file in Lahore.

If you live outside Pakistan, you can generally file through a relative or other person holding a **special power of attorney** in your favour, attested at a Pakistani embassy or consulate. Our page for [overseas Pakistanis](/overseas-pakistanis/) explains the attestation process.

## Documents you will need

Collect as many of these as you can before your first meeting with an advocate:

1. **Your CNIC** and, if available, a copy of your husband's CNIC.
2. **Nikahnama** (the marriage contract). The nikahnama records the haq mehr, any conditions agreed at the time of marriage, and whether the husband delegated the right of divorce to the wife (**talaq-e-tafweez**, clause 18). If you do not have a copy, it can usually be obtained from the Nikah Registrar or the Union Council.
3. **NADRA marriage registration certificate**, if one was issued.
4. **Children's B-forms or birth certificates**, if you are also claiming custody or maintenance.
5. **A list of dowry articles** (jahez) and gifts, with any receipts or photographs, if you are claiming their return.
6. **Your husband's current address** in Lahore or elsewhere, for service of the summons.
7. Any evidence relevant to other claims: messages, medical reports, police complaints or proof of the husband's income.

## Step 1: Consultation and drafting the suit

At the first meeting, the advocate will want to understand the history of the marriage, whether there are children, what was given as dower and dowry, and what you want to achieve. Many wives combine claims for **khula**, **dissolution on specific grounds**, **recovery of dowry articles**, **haq mehr**, **maintenance** for themselves (for the period allowed) and **maintenance for the children** in a single suit. Custody is usually claimed in a separate petition under the Guardians and Wards Act 1890.

The advocate then drafts the **plaint**, the written statement of your case. It sets out the facts, the reasons you can no longer live with your husband and the reliefs you seek. A clear plaint makes the rest of the case simpler. You sign it and verify it on oath.

## Step 2: Filing and service

The plaint, documents and court fee are filed in the Family Court. The court then issues **summons** to the husband. Under the Family Courts Act, service can be made by registered post, courier, SMS or other modern means, and if the husband cannot be found, by **publication** in a newspaper. Service is often the stage that takes longest, especially if the husband's address is uncertain.

## Step 3: The pre-trial hearing and reconciliation

Once the husband has been served, the court holds a **pre-trial hearing**. The Family Court must try to bring about reconciliation between the spouses. The judge may speak with both parties, sometimes privately, to see whether the marriage can be saved.

Under Section 10(4) of the West Pakistan Family Courts Act 1964, if reconciliation fails in a suit for dissolution of marriage, the court **shall pass a decree for dissolution of marriage forthwith**. In practice this means that in many khula cases, the decree is passed at the pre-trial stage, without a full trial, once the wife confirms she does not wish to continue the marriage.

If the husband does not appear despite proper service, the court can proceed **ex parte**, that is, in his absence.

## Step 4: Haq mehr and what the wife gives back

Because khula is the wife's request, the court may require her to return the benefits she received in exchange for the marriage, typically the haq mehr. Under the Punjab amendment to the Family Courts Act, when a decree for khula is passed, the court may direct the wife to surrender up to **fifty per cent of the deferred dower** or up to **twenty-five per cent of the admitted prompt dower**. The exact order depends on the facts and the court's assessment. If the dower was never paid, there may be nothing to return.

Other claims in the same suit, such as recovery of dowry articles or maintenance, do not end with the khula decree. If they are disputed, the court continues to hear them through evidence and arguments.

## Step 5: After the decree — Union Council and NADRA

A khula decree does not take full effect in the records on its own. Under Section 21 of the Family Courts Act, the court sends a certified copy of the decree to the **Chairman of the Union Council** concerned. The Union Council then treats the decree as notice of talaq under Section 7 of the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961. The dissolution becomes effective after **ninety days**, and the Union Council issues a certificate (often called the **effectiveness certificate** or divorce certificate).

You should:

- obtain a **certified copy** of the decree from the court;
- follow up with the Union Council where the marriage was registered or where you reside, to make sure the notice is processed;
- collect the **Union Council certificate** after the ninety-day period;
- apply to **NADRA** to update your marital status and obtain a divorce registration certificate.

These papers matter for remarriage, passports, visa applications and inheritance, so it is worth completing them carefully.

## Typical timeline

There is no fixed timetable, and we do not promise one. As a general guide:

- Drafting and filing: a few days after you provide the documents.
- Service on the husband: from two weeks to two months, depending on his address and whether publication is needed.
- Pre-trial hearing and decree: often at the first or second effective hearing after service.
- Union Council certificate: about three months after the decree is sent.

Disputed claims for dowry or maintenance can take longer. The husband can appeal against parts of the decree, but in khula cases the right of appeal is limited by law.

## Common questions about khula

**Is khula the same as divorce?** In everyday speech, people use "divorce" for both. Legally, talaq is pronounced by the husband and khula is granted by the court at the wife's request. The practical result, the end of the marriage, is the same.

**Will I lose custody of my children?** No. Khula does not decide custody. The welfare of the child is the court's main consideration in any custody case, and young children usually remain with their mother.

**Can my husband stop the khula?** He can attend court and present his side, and the court will attempt reconciliation, but he cannot veto the dissolution if the wife maintains that she cannot continue the marriage.
