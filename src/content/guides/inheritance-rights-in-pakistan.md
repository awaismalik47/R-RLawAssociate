Questions about **inheritance rights in Pakistan** usually arise at a difficult time: a parent, spouse or sibling has died, and the family has to decide what happens to the house, the land, the bank account and the business. This guide explains who the legal heirs are, how the main shares are worked out under Islamic law as applied in Pakistan, how the law protects women, and the practical steps to transfer inherited property. It is general information, not advice on a particular estate.

## The basic principle: inheritance opens on death

When a Muslim dies in Pakistan, their property passes to their **legal heirs** under Islamic law, as applied through the Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act 1962 and related laws. Three principles are worth understanding at the start:

1. **Ownership passes immediately.** Each legal heir becomes an owner of their share at the moment of death. A mutation or court order records that ownership; it does not create it.
2. **Debts come first.** Before the estate is divided, funeral expenses and the deceased's debts (including any unpaid haq mehr owed to a widow) are paid from it.
3. **Wills are limited.** A Muslim can leave a bequest (**wasiyat**) of up to one-third of the remaining estate, generally not to a legal heir unless the other heirs agree after the death. The rest is divided by fixed shares.

## Who the legal heirs are

The heirs most commonly involved are:

- **children**: sons and daughters;
- **the spouse**: widow or widower;
- **parents**: mother and father;
- in some cases, **brothers and sisters**, grandparents and more distant relatives, especially where there are no children.

Grandchildren whose parent died before the grandparent also have a right under **Section 4 of the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961**. They take the share that their parent would have received had they been alive. This is a Pakistani statutory rule that differs from the classical position, and it is often overlooked in family discussions.

## The main shares

The exact shares depend on who survives, and complicated families should always have the calculation checked. The most common shares under Sunni Hanafi law, which is followed by most Muslims in Pakistan, are:

| Heir | Share where the deceased left children | Share where there are no children |
|---|---|---|
| Widow (wife) | One-eighth (1/8), shared equally if more than one wife | One-quarter (1/4) |
| Widower (husband) | One-quarter (1/4) | One-half (1/2) |
| Mother | One-sixth (1/6) | One-third (1/3) in most cases, or 1/6 if there are two or more siblings |
| Father | One-sixth (1/6), plus any residue if there are only daughters | Takes as a residuary heir |
| Son(s) and daughter(s) together | The residue after the fixed shares, with each son taking twice the share of each daughter | — |
| Daughter(s) with no son | One daughter: one-half (1/2); two or more: two-thirds (2/3) shared | — |

**Example.** A man dies leaving a widow, one son and two daughters, and no surviving parents. The widow takes one-eighth. The remaining seven-eighths is divided so that the son receives twice what each daughter receives: the son takes 2/4 of the remainder and each daughter 1/4 of it. In fractions of the whole estate: widow 4/32, son 14/32, each daughter 7/32.

Shia (Jafari) law follows different rules for some heirs. If the deceased was Shia, the calculation should be done under that school.

## Women's inheritance rights

Islamic law gives women fixed, enforceable shares, but in practice women are often pressured to give them up, sometimes in exchange for the cost of their marriage or under the label of "family honour". Pakistani law provides several protections:

- **Section 498-A of the Pakistan Penal Code** makes it an offence to deprive a woman of her inheritance by deceitful or illegal means, punishable with imprisonment and a fine.
- In Punjab, the **Enforcement of Women's Property Rights Act 2021** allows a woman to complain to the **Ombudsperson** if she is deprived of ownership or possession of her property. The Ombudsperson can call for reports from the revenue and police authorities and pass orders.
- The courts examine **relinquishment deeds** and gift deeds by women in favour of male relatives very carefully, and they can be set aside if consent was not free and informed.
- A widow's unpaid **haq mehr** is a debt on the estate and must be paid before division.

## Step 1: Establish the legal heirs

The first practical step is to obtain documents proving the death and the family relationship:

- the **death certificate** from the Union Council or Cantonment Board;
- the **Family Registration Certificate (FRC)** from NADRA, which lists the family members registered with the deceased;
- CNICs of all legal heirs.

Where heirs are abroad or minors, special arrangements are needed. A minor's share is managed by a guardian, and a guardian appointed by the court may be required before the minor's property can be sold.

## Step 2: Succession certificate or letter of administration

For **movable assets**, such as bank accounts, shares, savings certificates, vehicles and pension dues, institutions usually require a formal document confirming who the heirs are.

- Under the **Letters of Administration and Succession Certificates Act 2020**, NADRA's **Succession Facilitation Units** can issue succession certificates and letters of administration where the heirs agree. This is often faster than going to court.
- Where the heirs disagree or the case is complex, an application is made to the **civil court** under the Succession Act 1925, and notices are published before the certificate is issued.

## Step 3: Transfer of immovable property

The process depends on the type of property:

- **Agricultural and rural land** recorded in the revenue record is transferred by **inheritance mutation (intiqal-e-wirasat)**. In Punjab this is processed through the Punjab Land Records Authority's service centres (Arazi Record Centres) on the basis of the death certificate, FRC and the heirs' statements.
- **Urban property** in housing societies or development authority schemes (such as LDA) is transferred in the authority's or society's own records on the basis of a succession document and the heirs' affidavits.
- **Registered urban property** outside a society may need the record updated with the relevant authority and, later, a registered deed if the heirs sell or partition.

Each heir's name should appear in proportion to their share. If a mutation is entered without an heir, or with the wrong shares, it can be challenged before the revenue authorities or in the civil court.

## Step 4: Dividing the property

Once the heirs are recorded as co-owners, they can divide the property by agreement (a **family settlement** or partition deed), sell it and divide the proceeds, or buy each other out. If they cannot agree, any co-owner can file a suit for **partition** in the civil court, or, for agricultural land, apply for partition before the revenue officer. Where the property cannot be divided conveniently, the court can order a sale and division of the price.

## Common problems

- **One heir in possession** refuses to share rent or allow a sale. A suit for partition and for accounts can be filed.
- **Old mutations** excluded daughters decades ago. These can often still be challenged, because co-ownership rights of heirs are given strong protection by the courts.
- **Gifts made shortly before death**, or while the owner was seriously ill, may be challenged as invalid if the legal requirements of a gift were not met.
- **Business assets** of a deceased partner raise both partnership law and succession issues.
