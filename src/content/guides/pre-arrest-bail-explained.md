**Pre-arrest bail in Pakistan** is a court order that protects a person from being arrested in a criminal case while the court examines whether the case against them is genuine. In Urdu it is called **zamanat qabl az giraftari**. This guide explains when it can be sought, how courts decide it, what happens at each hearing and how it differs from other kinds of bail. It is general information, not legal advice for your case.

## What pre-arrest bail is

Ordinary bail (post-arrest bail) is sought after the police have arrested someone. Pre-arrest bail is sought **before** arrest. It is an extraordinary remedy: the courts treat liberty as precious, but they also recognise that the police must be able to investigate crimes. Pre-arrest bail strikes a balance by protecting people from arrests that are made for an improper purpose.

The power to grant pre-arrest bail comes from **Section 498 of the Code of Criminal Procedure 1898 (CrPC)**. Over many decades, the superior courts of Pakistan have developed principles on when this power should be exercised.

## When courts grant pre-arrest bail

Courts have repeatedly held that pre-arrest bail is not to be used as a substitute for post-arrest bail in every case. Broadly, the accused has to show that:

1. **The case is mala fide or the arrest is intended for an ulterior motive.** For example, the FIR has been lodged to humiliate the accused, to pressure them in a property or family dispute, or for political reasons.
2. **There is a reasonable case on the merits.** Courts commonly consider whether the case falls within the "further inquiry" category under Section 497(2) CrPC, meaning that the evidence leaves real room for doubt about the accused's involvement.
3. **Arrest would cause irreparable harm** to the accused's reputation or liberty.

In recent years the Supreme Court of Pakistan has also observed that, in suitable cases, the merits of the case can be considered along with the question of mala fide when deciding pre-arrest bail. The weight given to each factor depends on the facts.

Factors that often count against pre-arrest bail include an offence that carries a very serious punishment, strong direct evidence such as an eyewitness account with supporting medical evidence, or a need for the police to recover something from the accused, such as a weapon or stolen property.

## Bailable and non-bailable offences

The Second Schedule of the CrPC classifies each offence as **bailable** or **non-bailable**.

- In a **bailable** offence, the accused has a right to be released on bail if they are arrested, so pre-arrest bail is usually not needed, although the police may still need to be dealt with.
- In a **non-bailable** offence, bail is at the court's discretion. Most pre-arrest bail petitions concern non-bailable offences.

Within non-bailable offences, Section 497 CrPC treats offences punishable with death, life imprisonment or ten years or more (the "prohibitory clause") more strictly than those carrying lesser punishments.

## Which court to approach

Both the **Court of Session** and the **High Court** have power to grant pre-arrest bail. As a matter of practice, a petitioner is normally expected to approach the Sessions Court first, unless there are exceptional circumstances. In Lahore, petitions are filed before the Sessions Judge, who usually sends them to an Additional Sessions Judge for hearing.

If the Sessions Court refuses, a fresh petition can be filed in the **Lahore High Court**. If the High Court refuses, a petition for leave to appeal can be filed in the **Supreme Court of Pakistan**.

## Protective bail

Sometimes a person is accused in a case registered in another district or province, and travelling there carries a real risk of arrest on the way. In these situations the High Court may grant **protective bail** (transitory bail) for a short period, allowing the person to reach the court that has jurisdiction and apply for pre-arrest bail there. Protective bail does not decide the case; it only gives safe passage to the proper court.

## Step by step: how a pre-arrest bail petition proceeds

### Step 1: Getting the FIR and preparing the petition

The advocate obtains a copy of the **FIR** (first information report) and any other available documents, such as earlier complaints or civil cases between the parties. The petition sets out the facts, why the case is false or exaggerated, and why arrest would be improper.

### Step 2: Personal appearance and ad-interim bail

The accused must appear **in person** before the court when the petition is filed. If the court finds that the matter needs examination, it usually grants **ad-interim pre-arrest bail**, a temporary order protecting the accused from arrest until the next date, on condition that they furnish **surety bonds** (a written guarantee by one or more sureties for a stated sum) and **join the investigation**.

### Step 3: Joining the investigation

After ad-interim bail, the accused is expected to cooperate with the investigating officer: appear when called, give their version and produce any evidence in their favour. Failure to join the investigation can lead the court to recall the bail.

### Step 4: Police report and the record

The court calls for the police record and a report on the progress of the investigation. The complainant usually appears through their own counsel to oppose bail.

### Step 5: Confirmation or recall

At the final hearing, the court hears both sides and either **confirms** the bail, meaning the accused remains protected until the case concludes (unless the bail is later cancelled), or **recalls** it. If the bail is recalled, the police can arrest the accused, who may then apply for post-arrest bail.

## What happens after bail is confirmed

Confirmation of pre-arrest bail is not an acquittal. The investigation continues, and if a **challan** (police report under Section 173 CrPC) is submitted, the case goes to trial. The accused must attend every date of the trial; absence without a good reason can lead to cancellation of bail and warrants of arrest.

Bail can also be cancelled later under Section 497(5) CrPC if, for example, the accused misuses the bail by threatening witnesses.

## Practical tips

- **Act quickly but carefully.** It is better to approach the court before the police arrive at your door, but a poorly prepared petition can do more harm than good.
- **Keep all papers.** Bring any document that shows the background of the dispute: civil suits, earlier complaints, messages and photographs.
- **Attend every hearing on time.** Courts take non-appearance very seriously in bail matters.
- **Do not contact the complainant or witnesses** about the case without your advocate's advice.
- **Coordinate with any related case.** Criminal cases often run alongside family, property or service disputes, and what you say in one may be used in another.
